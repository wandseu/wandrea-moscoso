Commerce Desk

An operations tool for a marketplace where crew settle purchases against their allotment — payment as a payroll event, not a card transaction. I designed and built the front-end that replaced spreadsheets and developer requests.

Context

The platform sells to ship crew and their families. Crew are paid partly through allotment — a fixed share of wages routed home to a designated allottee — so an order can be settled by deducting it from that allotment instead of paying up front. Crew and allottees can also register as merchants and sell on the same platform.

One person can therefore be a buyer, an allottee and a seller at once, and payment is a payroll event rather than a card transaction. The admin system has to make all of that legible to a small operations team.

The system shipped internally as Merchant Portal — a name describing one of six user types and none of the catalog, order or deduction work it actually does. I've presented it here as Commerce Desk: what the team gained was a place to run the business from.

The design question

How do you give operations direct control of the data they own — and build something they'd genuinely switch to, rather than keep open beside the spreadsheet?

The problem

The people who understood the business couldn't act on it.

Operations ran on spreadsheets

Product lists, order tracking and merchant records were maintained by hand, in files that drifted out of sync with the actual database.

Anything system-level needed a developer

Changing a price or correcting an order meant filing a request and waiting for someone to edit the record on the backend. Slow for routine work, and no record of who changed what.

Deductions had no home

A commitment against future wages spans payroll cycles and can be interrupted by a contract ending. No off-the-shelf ecommerce admin has a pattern for that.

Design decisions

01

Designing for spreadsheet users

The users weren't resistant to software — they were fluent in a different one. Their mental model was rows, columns, filters and copy-paste, and a tool that fought that model would lose to the spreadsheet, because the spreadsheet already worked.

  • Dense tables, not card grids. Compact rows, sticky headers, alignment that mirrors how ops would lay it out themselves.
  • Inline editing on high-frequency fields. Clicking through to a detail page to change one number is exactly the friction that sends people back to Excel.
  • Bulk select and bulk actions. Spreadsheet users think in ranges, not individual records.
  • CSV import/export as a bridge. An escape hatch made adoption feel low-risk while trust was still being built.
Where is this order's money?
02

Allotment deduction as a first-class flow

A card payment succeeds or fails in a moment. An allotment deduction is a commitment against future wages, spread across payroll cycles, and it can be disrupted by things that have nothing to do with the order — a contract ending, a crew member disembarking, a change in allotment amount.

So the order detail carries the schedule itself: the plan laid out by cycle with per-cycle status, a per-buyer commitment summary so over-commitment is visible before it becomes a collection problem, and flagging for schedules a contract end will interrupt.

Order status and deduction status are different things, kept visually and verbally separate — an order can be delivered while its deductions are still running.

03

One person, several roles

A person record couldn't be a flat customer row. Buying history and merchant activity live in one view but in role-specific sections, with merchant approval kept as its own workflow — so nobody mistakes a storefront's metrics for that person's own purchases.

04

Turning developer tasks into forms

Every field a developer used to edit by hand became a question of what rules should exist around it: validation on prices, change history, post-placement edits constrained to what's safe, and confirmation friction proportional to consequence. Replacing a developer isn't giving ops raw database access with a nicer font — it's encoding the judgement the developer was applying.

The design system

Calm chrome, dense working surfaces.

A lot of dashboard design puts air everywhere, which photographs well and slows people down on the fiftieth record of the day. So the system splits in two: sidebar, headers and summary cards get generous spacing and soft shadows — that's where someone lands and decides whether the tool is trustworthy. Tables, order lists and deduction schedules get compressed rows and tighter padding, because there density is the courtesy. Same tokens, different rhythm.

Colour

Yellow is deliberately scarce — one accent per screen, so the eye always knows where the primary action is. Used twice it becomes decorative and worthless.

Status language
Pending In progress Collected At risk Failed

Order status, deduction status and merchant approval share one palette, so a colour carries one meaning system-wide. Status is never carried by colour alone — ops read these under time pressure, and some print them.

Numerals
₱ 8,100
₱ 32,400
₱241,900

Tabular numerals everywhere. Prices, quantities and allotment amounts have to align down a column or the table stops being scannable — a small decision that matters more here than in most products.

Front-end implementation

Plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript — no framework. For an interface this table-heavy that meant deliberately building my own foundations rather than importing them.

Honest tradeoff: without a framework, state consistency is manual work. Keeping a filtered table in sync after an inline edit was the recurring problem, and the answer was to make the table module the single owner of its row state rather than patching the DOM at the call site.

  • A reusable data table module. Server-side pagination, sorting, filtering, bulk selection — shared across products, orders, merchants and promos instead of rewritten per screen.
  • Event delegation on table containers. Rows re-render without rebinding hundreds of listeners.
  • Shared modal, toast and validation modules. Every screen fails the same way.
  • One fetch wrapper. Auth, error shapes and loading states handled in a single place; debounced search keeps large lists responsive.

Adoption is the honest measure here rather than a percentage: the spreadsheets stopped being updated, which is the outcome the project existed for.

What I'd do differently

  • Build the shared table module before the third screen instead of after — the first two still carry inconsistencies.
  • Sit with ops during a real workday earlier. The most useful decisions came from watching, not from requirements.
  • Design the deduction states before the order states — deduction is the harder concept, and order status ended up bending around it anyway.
