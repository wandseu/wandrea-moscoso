FundMyFuture

Turning a mandatory salary allotment into a plan for the future — a wage-planning app for seafarers that reframes the legally required 80% family remittance from money sent away into money put to work.

Role
Sole designer — research through final UI
Team
Direct with the owner & executive team
Timeline
4 months · Jan – Apr 2025
Status
Launched to crew
80%

Context

Under the Magna Carta of Filipino Seafarers, at least 80% of a seafarer's monthly wage must be remitted to their designated family allottee. It is not optional and not negotiable — it is the single largest fixed movement of money in a seafarer's financial life.

What the law guarantees is that the money arrives. What it says nothing about is what happens next. The remaining 20% still has to absorb government deductions, loan repayments, subscriptions and shipboard allowance — while the 80% lands ashore and is spent as it comes, on real and legitimate needs, with no structure behind it.

The project began as the personal initiative of the company's owner, who wanted crew to end their contracts with something to show for them.

The design question

How might we help seafarers see their whole salary at once, and give the money they're already required to send a job to do?

The problem

Seafarers earn well. Many still retire with nothing.

The gap isn't income — it's infrastructure. Research surfaced three compounding issues.

No visibility

Crew knew their gross salary and roughly what landed at home, but few could account for the middle. Deductions, amortizations and subscriptions were understood individually and never as a whole.

No separation of family money and future money

The 80% arrived as one undifferentiated sum. Money for a house and money for groceries were indistinguishable in the same account — and short-term needs always won.

No product that matched the goal

Savings options existed but were framed in industry terms — tenor, yield, risk profile. Crew were thinking in human terms: a house, tuition, a hospital fund, the year they stop sailing.

Research

Eight interviews with active and returning crew across ranks, plus three sessions with allottees ashore.

Working alongside the owner and executives gave me direct access to crew and to the company's payroll reality — I could design against actual deduction structures rather than assumptions.

Constraints that shaped everything after

  • Connectivity is scarce and expensive at sea. The app had to be useful in short, low-bandwidth sessions and readable offline.
  • The money is managed by someone else. The allottee ashore makes most day-to-day decisions, so a plan the seafarer sets alone will not hold.
  • Contracts are finite and repeating. Planning follows a 6–9 month contract rhythm, not a calendar year.
  • Financial vocabulary is a barrier. Not a literacy problem — a translation problem.

The reframe

Not where the money went — what it's now doing.

Most budgeting apps start from where did my money go? For this crew that question was already answered by law: 80% went home. Starting there would have produced an app that told people what they already knew. So the product starts one step later — and that single shift determined the whole structure: allocation first, then goals, then growth.

Design decisions

01

The salary breakdown as one continuous view

Rather than separate screens for deductions, loans and allotment, one flow follows a single month's salary from gross to its final resting places: government deductions, loans, subscriptions, shipboard allowance, personal allocation, family allocation.

Crew could finally see the whole month in one motion — and, critically, see how much room actually existed to redirect.

A house in 5 years
02

Goal-based programs, not financial products

Programs are surfaced by what the money is for — housing, education, retirement, health — and each goal is matched to a vehicle suited to its time horizon and liquidity needs. The mechanics sit underneath; the goal sits on top.

A user chooses "a house in five years," not a five-year time deposit.

03

Everything editable from the ship

Allottee details, allocation split, loans and deductions all sit one tap from the profile — no office ticket, no waiting for shore hours.

Circumstances change mid-contract. A plan you cannot edit is a plan you abandon.

04

Progressive disclosure over dense forms

Deductions, then allowance, then goals — never all three at once. Each step of setup asks one question and shows its effect on the total before moving on.

05

Designing for the ship, not the desk

Large touch targets, high-contrast type, minimal per-session data load, and a layout that holds up on older devices in poor lighting. Every core screen answers its question above the fold.

Visual language

Financial apps default to a narrow register: royal blue, dense data, tight rows. This product's emotional register is long-term and steady rather than transactional, so the surface is calmer. Teal over category-standard blue, deliberately — it reads as growth rather than banking, which is the entire argument of the product.

Neutral canvas, white cards

Soft off-white pages with pure white cards separated by surface contrast alone — no borders. 16px radii on hero surfaces, 12px on standard cards.

One accent, with discipline

Deep teal carries every point of emphasis: the allotment hero, active navigation, every goal's progress fill. Everything else is black, gray or white.

Numerals are the typography

Figures are large, tightly tracked, medium weight; labels stay small and quiet. Two weights only — size does the emphasis work.

Category colour, twice

Icon tile backgrounds and progress fills only. Housing, education, health and retirement each own a hue — never as text, never as a full card fill.

Generous vertical rhythm

20px inside hero cards, 16–18px inside standard cards. A screen about someone's retirement should not feel like a spreadsheet.

Motion as explanation

For users who found financial products opaque, animation wasn't polish — it was the mechanism that made the math legible. Four micro-interactions carry the salary breakdown, and every one runs locally at zero network cost.

Figures count, not jump

Animating gross to net lets the user see subtraction happen. A number that snaps has to be re-read; one that travels tells you which direction it went.

The bar reallocates, never redraws

Segments grow and shrink inside a fixed track, so the total stays visibly constant. The most important interaction in the app — the visual argument that the 80% didn't disappear, it moved.

Line items stagger in

Roughly 70ms apart. It reads as the system working through the math line by line, and it gives the eye a reading order instead of dumping six rows at once.

One question per step

Deductions, then allowance, then goals — never all three at once. Progressive disclosure across the entire setup flow.

Testing

I presented the concept and a working demo to the executive team and to crew members for hands-on testing.

  • Easy to use and easy to understand — the breakdown made sense without explanation
  • Crew valued being able to update their own details
  • Seeing the full salary breakdown in one place was the moment the concept landed
  • That the 80% could grow rather than simply arrive was the strongest source of enthusiasm

Outcome

FundMyFuture launched to the crew.

Adoption figures are still being gathered post-launch, so the qualitative findings stand on their own here — an honest case study beats an inflated one.

Reflection

The constraint everyone treated as the problem was the opportunity.

The hardest part of this project wasn't the interface. A guaranteed monthly transfer is the closest thing to a reliable savings mechanism a person can have. It just needed somewhere to go.

What I'd explore next

  • An allottee-facing view, so the family ashore participates in the plan rather than receiving it
  • Progress framing tied to contract cycles rather than calendar months
  • Post-launch research on whether goal-based framing changes actual saving behaviour over a full contract
